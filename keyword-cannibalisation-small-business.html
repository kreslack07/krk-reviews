Keyword Cannibalisation: What Google Actually Says, and When to Fix It

Published 2026-11-22 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

The phrase is not in Google's documentation

Search the term “keyword cannibalisation” across Google Search Central and you get nothing. Google documents the same ground under three other headings: duplicate or very similar pages and canonicalisation, the site diversity system, and its guidance on consolidating duplicate URLs. That gap matters, because it is where the scare stories live: the idea that two pages on one topic will get you penalised.

What the documents actually say

The SEO Starter Guide puts it flatly under “Reduce duplicate content”: having duplicate content on your site “is not a violation of our spam policies”, though it “can be a bad user experience and search engines might waste crawling resources on URLs that you don't even care about”. Google's canonicalisation overview repeats that some duplicate content on a site is normal and not a spam-policy violation, and adds the line worth remembering: indicating a canonical preference is “a hint, not a rule”.

The ranking systems guide describes the site diversity system, which “generally won't show more than two web page listings from the same site in our top results” — and then the exception: Google “may still show more than two listings in cases where our systems determine it's especially relevant to do so”. Subdomains are generally treated as part of the root domain for that test.

So when is it a real problem?

Not a compliance problem, ever. A performance problem, sometimes: when several pages serve the same intent for the same query, they split the clicks and the links instead of concentrating them. Two pages answering genuinely different questions — “how much does it cost” and “how do I install it” — are not competing, and can both rank.

The 20-minute diagnosis

  1. Open Search Console → Performance → Queries. Pick the ten queries with the most impressions.
  2. Click each query, switch to the Pages tab, and note every URL that receives impressions for it.
  3. Where more than one URL appears, open each and ask a single question: same intent or different? If a visitor clicking either page would be equally satisfied, it is the same intent.
  4. Write the answer down next to the URL pair. That written decision is the deliverable — not a merge.

Fixing the same-intent pairs

Google's guidance is to redirect non-preferred URLs to the one URL that best represents the information, and to use rel="canonical" only if you cannot redirect. The method order in Google's consolidate-duplicate-URLs page: redirects are the strongest signal that the target should become canonical, rel="canonical" link annotations are a strong signal, and sitemap inclusion is weaker. That page also says not to use robots.txt or noindex to do this job — noindex removes the page from Search entirely rather than passing its signals on.

Different-intent pairs need no redirect at all. Sharpen the title and opening paragraph of each so the two pages stop reading like the same offer, and interlink them so a reader can move between them.
