Title Tags and Meta Descriptions: What Google Rewrites, and Why

Published 2026-11-21 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

Two lines are visible in a search result before anyone clicks: the title link and the snippet. You write both in the page source, and Google can replace both when its own text is judged to represent the page better.

This is the on-page half of our 48-hour AI & SEO website audit, written out so you can check it yourself.

A title element is a suggestion, not the title

Google's guide to title links describes the title link as automated, and built from many sources: content in the <title> element, the main visual title on the page, heading elements such as <h1>, the og:title meta tag, other large and prominent text, anchor text, and WebSite structured data.

That list explains a common complaint: a site edits its title element and the search result keeps the old wording. Google has to recrawl and reprocess the page before a change appears, which the same documentation puts at days to weeks. Requesting a recrawl speeds that along, but the title element is never the sole input.

How to write a title that survives the rewrite

The documented practices are short:

  • Give every page a title in the <title> element.
  • Keep the text descriptive and concise; avoid a vague label such as "Home" on the home page.
  • Avoid keyword stuffing, because repeating the same terms makes a result look spammy.
  • Avoid repeated or boilerplate titles across pages, since identical titles make pages indistinguishable.
  • Brand concisely — the site name and a delimiter, rather than a long slogan on every page.

There is no official length limit, but the title link is truncated to fit the device, so the useful part belongs at the front.

When Google substitutes its own title

The same guide lists the cases where a title link may be built from the page instead of from your markup: a half-empty title element, an obsolete title on a page that is reused and never updated, an inaccurate title, boilerplate titles shared across pages, a page with no clear main title, a title in a different writing system from the page, and a title that duplicates the site name.

Each of those is a template bug rather than a wording problem, which is why title faults repeat across a whole section of a site.

Snippets and meta descriptions

A snippet is created from the page content, and Google will use the <meta name="description"> tag when it describes the page better than the surrounding text. The guidance asks for a unique description on each page, notes that site-level descriptions belong on the main page, and confirms that there is no limit on length even though the displayed snippet is truncated.

Write it as a plain summary in the words a customer would use; it is also the text an AI assistant tends to read before deciding whether the page answers.

What to check on your own site

Open the source of your home page and two service pages. Confirm each carries a title element and a meta description, that the wording differs between them, and that none of the issues above apply. Then read the title Google is actually serving for the same URLs: a rewritten title or a missing description points at the template rather than the copy.
