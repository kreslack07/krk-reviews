Scaled Content Abuse: How Many AI-Assisted Pages Is Too Many?

Published 2026-11-26 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

Publishing blocks are cheap now. One brief, one model, and twenty suburb pages exist by Friday. Google's spam policies were rewritten for exactly that pattern, and the wording is worth reading before you scale up.

What the policy actually says

“Scaled content abuse is when many pages are generated for the primary purpose of manipulating search rankings and not helping users. This abusive practice is typically focused on creating large amounts of unoriginal content that provides little to no value to users, no matter how it's created.”

That last clause is the part people miss. This is not an AI policy. The examples Google lists include using generative AI tools to produce many pages without adding value, scraping feeds or search results, “automated transformations like synonymizing, translating, or other obfuscation techniques”, stitching together content from different pages, spreading pages across multiple sites to hide the scale, and pages where the text “makes little or no sense to a reader but contains search keywords”. A person hand-writing two hundred templated pages is judged by the same rule.

There is no page-count threshold

Nothing in the policy states a number of pages, a publishing rate, or an AI-content percentage. Google does not publish one, so any consultant quoting a limit is inventing it. The test is purpose and value per page. A ten-page site where six pages exist to catch keyword variants is abusive at that size; a three-hundred-page resource library where each page answers a distinct question with original detail is not.

The sibling risk: site reputation

The site reputation policy covers third-party content published on a host site “mainly because of that host's already-established ranking signals”. If you accept guest posts, partner content or a white-label blog, that content is judged on whether it belongs on your domain — not on whether a contract exists.

What enforcement looks like

Google detects violations “both through automated systems and, as needed, human review that can result in a manual action”, and sites that violate the policies “may rank lower in results or not appear in results at all”. The policy's own remedy for scaled content already on a site is blunt: “exclude it from Search”. In practice that means noindex or removal, not a rewrite — which is why the decision is cheaper to make before publishing than after.

A scaling test that holds up

  • Every page answers one question a real customer asked, with detail that only your business has — prices, suburbs, lead times, photos, equipment, staff.
  • Every page survives the swap test: if replacing the town name would make two pages identical, they are one page with two URLs.
  • The pages link to each other and back to the service hub along a route a visitor could follow, instead of sitting as a flat block of near-duplicates.
  • Pages that cannot meet the bar get noindex rather than being published anyway. Crawled-but-worthless pages consume crawl budget and blur the signals that years of real work created.

Run it as an experiment, not a bet: publish a batch of ten, wait for indexing, then compare Search Console per-page impressions against the queries your hub page already earns. Keep the pages that bring genuinely new queries; noindex the rest. That is reversible page by page, unlike an all-in publishing spree you can only undo by deleting your own library.
