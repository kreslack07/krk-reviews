Local Business Schema Markup: What AI Search Actually Reads

Published 2026-11-21 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

Every page already says something to a search engine: a title, a heading, a phone number in the footer. Structured data is that same information written so a machine can read it without inferring. For a local business the block that carries the most weight is LocalBusiness, and the gap it closes is simple — it states what you are, where you work and how you can be reached, instead of leaving a crawler to guess from prose.

This is the schema check inside our 48-hour AI & SEO website audit, written out so you can run it yourself.

The fields that do the work

Google's documentation for LocalBusiness structured data and the schema.org LocalBusiness type both list far more properties than a service business needs. The ones that change what a search engine or an AI assistant can answer with:

  • name, url and image — the identity of the business.
  • telephone and email — contact details a machine can quote directly.
  • address as a full PostalAddress — street, suburb, state, postcode, country.
  • geo — latitude and longitude, if you are comfortable publishing them.
  • areaServed — the suburbs or regions you genuinely cover.
  • openingHoursSpecification — your hours, in a machine-readable form.
  • sameAs — links to your Google Business Profile and other verified profiles.
  • priceRange — a short band, never a quote for a job.

Matching the page is the rule that voids markup

Google's general structured data guidelines require the markup to describe content a visitor can see on that page. Markup claiming a Sydney office on a page with an Adelaide address, reviews that are not displayed, or hours that closed two years ago is not a shortcut — it is a mismatch, and it can cost you the rich result entirely. Change the page and the JSON-LD in the same edit.

Place the block once per page, inside a <script type="application/ld+json"> tag in the head. Use the Rich Results Test to check what Google extracts, and the Schema Markup Validator to check the syntax against the vocabulary itself. Both are free, and between them they catch nearly every mistake.

What AI answer engines read

An AI assistant answering "who does this near me" is looking for agreement between sources. A consistent business name, address and phone across your site, your Google Business Profile and the directories you are listed in is what makes a business quotable. Conflicting details make citing you risky, so the answer engine leans toward a business whose details match everywhere. sameAs links tell the machine which profiles are yours instead of leaving it to decide.

What a self-check will not do

You can validate syntax yourself in ten minutes. What a validator cannot tell you is which of your pages carry no markup at all, whether the copy on the page gives a machine anything worth quoting, or whether your location details are fighting each other across a dozen directory listings.
