Breadcrumb Markup: the Cheapest Crawl-Depth and Rich-Result Win on a Small Site

Published 2026-11-25 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

Breadcrumbs are usually filed under design nicety. On a small business site they do two jobs at once: they describe the path Google took to reach a page, and they are one of the few structured data types that can change how your listing looks. Both are cheap to get right, and both are easy to get subtly wrong in a way that never shows up as an error.

What Google does with the markup

Google Search “uses breadcrumb markup in the body of a web page to categorize the information from the page in search results”. The visible trail and the BreadcrumbList JSON-LD are separate artefacts. The trail is for people, and its links are crawlable. The markup is what makes the trail eligible for a rich result in Google's listing, where the trail replaces the raw URL.

The minimum viable BreadcrumbList

Google's definition is blunt: a BreadcrumbList must contain “at least two ListItems”. Each ListItem needs:

  • item — the URL that represents that breadcrumb. It is optional for the last item only; if you leave it out, Google uses the URL of the containing page.
  • name — the title shown to the user.
  • position — an integer, where 1 signifies the beginning of the trail.

Three items is the sane shape — category, sub-category, page — and the trail should describe a route a person would actually take. Google's recommendation is explicit: provide breadcrumbs that “represent a typical user path to a page, instead of mirroring the URL structure”, and it confirms you need no ListItem for the top-level path (your domain or host name), nor for the page itself. Where several routes lead to the same page, multiple breadcrumb trails on one page are allowed.

Where the crawl-depth win comes from

The structured data buys eligibility for a display treatment. Google is clear that it does not guarantee rich results will appear, so treat the markup as a cheap bet rather than a ranking lever. The visible trail is the part that earns its keep: it places contextual internal links on every page, pointing up through your hierarchy.

On a site whose only inbound internal links are the main navigation and the sitemap, category and service hub pages are often the pages with the fewest links pointing at them — and internal links are how Google discovers and re-crawls a URL in the first place. Breadcrumbs are the cheapest way to thicken that path without rebuilding navigation.

Two rules keep it honest. Link only to URLs that return 200 and are indexable; breadcrumb links pointing at redirects, 404s or noindexed pages waste crawl budget and muddy canonical signals. And never mark up a trail that is not on the page — structured data with no visible equivalent is a policy problem, not a shortcut.

Validate, then watch the report

Run the Rich Results Test, fix the critical errors, then monitor Search Console's Breadcrumb rich result report. Two signals are worth acting on: an increase in invalid items after a template change, which usually means a rolled-out template broke the markup, and a decrease in valid items not matched by an increase in invalid ones, which means the markup stopped being emitted at all. Allow several days for re-crawling before treating any report as final.
