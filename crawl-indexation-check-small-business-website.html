Indexed or Invisible? A 20-Minute Crawl and Indexation Check for Your Small-Business Website

Published 2026-11-21 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

There are three separate stages between publishing a page and a customer finding it: crawling (Google fetches the URL), indexing (Google stores and understands it), and ranking (Google decides where it appears for a search). A page can fail at any one of them, and the failure looks identical from the outside — nobody arrives. Twenty minutes with a browser and Search Console separates the three.

This is the crawl-and-indexation check from our 48-hour AI & SEO website audit, written out in the open. Run it on your three most valuable pages before you spend anything on content or ads.

Check 1 — Is the page even findable? (2 minutes)

Search Google for the exact URL:


site:yourdomain.com.au/your-page/

If the page comes back, Google knows about it. If nothing comes back for that URL — and site:yourdomain.com.au returns nothing at all — then your site is not indexed, and no amount of keyword work will help yet. For a brand-new site, Google's own guidance is that a week or so of crawling is normal before pages start appearing.

Then search for a phrase you wrote on that page, in quotes. A page that is indexed but cannot be found for its own sentences has a content or relevance problem, not an indexing problem. Write down which of the two you have, because they need completely different fixes.

Check 2 — robots.txt: the file that says "do not look" (4 minutes)

Open yourdomain.com.au/robots.txt. It should be a short, boring file. What you are looking for is a block like this left behind by a developer or agency while the site was in staging:


User-agent: *
Disallow: /

That single line tells every crawler to abandon the whole site. It is one of the most common causes of a business site that ranks for nothing and nobody can explain why.

Two things about robots.txt that are widely misunderstood, and both are straight from Google's documentation:

  • robots.txt is not a way to keep a page out of Google. A URL disallowed in robots.txt can still be indexed if other sites link to it — it will just be surfaced without a description. Google's own words: it is "not a mechanism for keeping a web page out of Google."
  • Specifying noindex in robots.txt does not work. Google does not support it. If you want a page out of the index, use the noindex rule, in a meta tag or an X-Robots-Tag HTTP header. That distinction is spelled out on the block indexing with noindex page.

So a second thing to check: view the page source and confirm there is no <meta name="robots" content="noindex"> in the <head>. This arrives on small-business sites in two ways — a leftover staging setting, and a page-builder or theme toggle named something like "hide from search engines" that somebody flipped while testing. X-Robots-Tag: noindex in the response headers does the same damage and is invisible in page source; check the headers, not just the HTML.

Check 3 — sitemap.xml hygiene (3 minutes)

Your sitemap.xml should exist, return HTTP 200, and list the URLs you actually want indexed — not draft pages, not tag archives, not the thank-you page a customer lands on after buying. Look for:

  • Stale lastmod dates. If every entry still says the year the site launched, the sitemap is being ignored more often than it is being used.
  • Missing pages. A page that is live but absent from the sitemap has to be discovered through an internal link instead. Add it.
  • Submitted, but not fetched. Submitting a sitemap in Search Console returns an acceptance, not a crawl. Search Console reports lastSubmitted and lastDownloaded separately, and a download date of "never" is the honest answer to whether Google has read it.

A sitemap is hygiene, not a lever. It helps Google find pages. It does not make them rank.

Check 4 — canonicals and duplicate URLs (4 minutes)

Search Console's Page Indexing report commonly shows "Duplicate without user-selected canonical". The causes are mechanical:

  • www and non-www both serving the site.
  • http and https both reachable.
  • Trailing slash and non-trailing slash variants of the same page.
  • Shop and product-URL parameters generating one page per filter or sort order.

Pick one canonical form, redirect every variant to it with a single permanent redirect, and set a canonical tag (<link rel="canonical" href="...">) on each page pointing at the chosen form. Then check that the canonical is self-referencing — the most common canonical error is a template that points every page at the homepage.

Check 5 — status codes and redirect hops (3 minutes)

  • 200 is the goal for a page you want indexed.
  • 301 means a permanent move. One hop is fine; four chained hops slow crawling and dilute signals.
  • 302 is temporary. Do not use it for a permanent move.
  • 404 and 410 are legitimate for pages you have removed — not indexation errors.
  • Soft 404s are the sneaky one: a page that returns HTTP 200 but shows "no results" or an empty category. Google treats it as a missing page even though the status code says otherwise.

A redirect chain is also a real speed problem: every hop is another round trip before the HTML starts arriving, which also shows up in how long the page takes to paint its main content.

Check 6 — are AI crawlers allowed in? (2 minutes)

If a meaningful share of your enquiries starts with "I asked ChatGPT" or "Perplexity said", your visibility depends on crawlers beyond Googlebot. Each company publishes its own user-agent tokens — GPTBot and OAI-SearchBot from OpenAI, ClaudeBot from Anthropic, PerplexityBot — and robots.txt is where you either allow them or shut them out.

The failure mode is copy-paste: a robots.txt rule block written to stop "AI training" scrapers that also stops the crawlers that fetch pages to cite you in an answer. Blocking the training crawler is a legitimate business decision. Blocking the retrieval crawlers is a decision to be absent from AI answers. Decide deliberately, per token, rather than in bulk — and remember that user-triggered fetches (such as OpenAI's ChatGPT-User) may not follow robots.txt at all.

Check 7 — read the actual report (2 minutes)

In Search Console, open Indexing → Pages. Every URL is either indexed or excluded with a reason, and the reasons mean different things:

  • "Discovered – currently not indexed" — Google knows the URL, has not fetched it yet. Usually a crawl-priority or site-quality signal, common on new sites.
  • "Crawled – currently not indexed" — Google fetched it and chose not to index it. A content or duplication question, not a technical block.
  • "Excluded by 'noindex' tag" — go back to Check 2.
  • "Blocked by robots.txt" — go back to Check 2 as well, and decide whether that block is intentional.
  • "Page with redirect" — expected for variants you consolidated, a problem if it is your money page.

Google's own guidance for sites with fewer than a few hundred pages is that the site: queries in Check 1 are usually enough, and the report is for when they come back empty. If a page is clean but has simply not been crawled, Search Console's URL Inspection tool lets you request indexing — one page at a time, and it is a request, not a guarantee.

The order that matters

Fix in this sequence: a Disallow: / or a stray noindex outranks everything else, because nothing else you do can work while the page is blocked. Then canonical and redirect consolidation. Then sitemap hygiene and AI-crawler permissions. Then content — which is the point at which keyword work starts to pay.
