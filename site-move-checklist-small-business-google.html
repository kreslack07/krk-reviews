Moving Your Website to a New Domain or CMS: Google's Documented Checklist

Published 2026-11-28 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

A site move is the highest-risk change a small business makes: a new domain, a new CMS, HTTP to HTTPS, or a new URL pattern. Google publishes its own version of the checklist, and most of the traffic lost during Australian site rebuilds is lost on steps it names in plain language.

Change one thing at a time

Site moves and migrations starts with general best practices. Plan changes one after another, not together: moving domain, changing CMS and refreshing the layout are three moves, done in sequence. Time the move to a recurring traffic dip so fewer users meet any problems, and expect ‘temporary fluctuation in site ranking during the move’. For a medium-sized website, Google says it can take ‘a few weeks or more’ before new URLs replace old ones, and the speed depends on how many URLs you have and how fast your server answers. Small and medium sites are advised to move all URLs at the same time so the update is detected faster; large sites can go section by section.

Map old URLs to new ones before anything goes live

The mapping is the work. Google’s suggestions for finding your old URLs: your sitemaps, the URLs that get the most traffic in your analytics or server logs, and the Links report in Search Console. Include images, video, JavaScript and CSS files — they move like everything else. Then, on the new site, every URL needs a self-referencing rel="canonical" pointing at the new address; hreflang annotations must be updated if you use them; and internal links must be rewritten to the new URLs. Keep two lists ready for go-live: a sitemap of the new URLs, and a list of the sites linking to the old ones.

Redirects: server-side, direct, and kept for a year

Use server-side permanent redirects — 301 or 308. Googlebot will follow up to ten hops in a chain, but the guide advises redirecting straight to the final destination and keeping any chain to no more than three. Client-side redirects are a last resort only. And this part surprises people: 301s do not cause a loss in PageRank. Keep the redirects in place ‘as long as possible, generally at least 1 year’, because that is how long signal transfer and link re-pointing can take.

The two steps people skip

First, the Change of Address tool is only for a domain or subdomain change. You do not need it for HTTP to HTTPS, for switching between www and non-www on the same domain, or for moving paths within a domain — and filing it when it is not applicable wastes time. Second, verification: verify every variant of both properties (www and non-www, http and https) before the move, keep your HTML verification file in the new copy, and submit the new sitemap once the redirects are on. Google also advises cleaning a newly acquired domain of the previous owner’s manual actions and leftover URL removals, removing any development-time noindex or robots.txt blocks, and making sure the new server has capacity — Google crawls harder right after a migration. Content you are not moving should return 404 or 410, not a soft page.
