Canonical Tags and Duplicate URLs: Which Page Should Google Rank?

Published 2026-11-21 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

Most small sites publish more URLs than pages. The home page answers at / and at /index.html; the same content is reachable over http:// and https://, with and without www, with a tracking parameter bolted on, and sometimes from a staging copy the host left crawlable. Google must pick one of those URLs to show people, and its pick is the canonical. You can state a preference: Google's canonicalisation guide ranks the methods by how strongly each influences that choice.

Three signals, in order of strength

  • Redirects — strongest. Use a permanent redirect only when you are genuinely retiring a duplicate, because the target becomes the canonical.
  • rel="canonical" annotations — a <link rel="canonical"> in the <head>, or the same instruction as an HTTP header, which also covers non-HTML files such as PDFs.
  • Sitemap inclusion — weakest. It helps, but Google must still work out which URL it duplicates.

None of this is compulsory. Say nothing and Google selects a canonical itself. The methods stack, so combining two or more increases the chance your preferred URL is the one displayed.

Four reasons to say it out loud

Choosing the URL people see; consolidating link signals into one URL; getting one set of metrics per piece of content; and not burning crawl time on pages you did not want crawled.

The instructions that quietly get ignored

  • robots.txt is not a canonicalisation tool — a blocked URL can still be indexed without its content.
  • Neither is the URL removal tool; it hides every version of the URL from Search.
  • Do not point one page at two different canonicals using two different methods.
  • Do not canonicalise to a URL fragment; Google does not support fragments here.
  • Do not use noindex to choose between your own pages — it removes the page from Search, which is why rel="canonical" is the recommended tool.
  • Do include a self-referencing canonical on the canonical page itself.
  • Use absolute URLs (https://example.com/page), not paths (/page); relative canonicals break the day a staging site gets crawled.
  • A rel="canonical" carrying hreflang, lang, media or type is ignored.
  • Link between your own pages using the canonical URL, not a duplicate.

Check what Google actually chose

Search Console's URL Inspection tool shows two fields side by side: the canonical you declared and the one Google selected. Where they disagree, one of the instructions above has been overruled.

A twenty-minute pass

  1. Inspect the home page and your three most valuable URLs. Note any Google-selected canonical that is not the URL you would print on a business card.
  2. Search each page source for more than one link rel="canonical".
  3. Check that the http and www variants genuinely 301 to the version you declared.
  4. Look for parameter URLs in the index (?utm_, ?gclid, shop sort and filter parameters) and canonicalise or block them properly.
  5. Confirm the sitemap lists canonical URLs and nothing else.
