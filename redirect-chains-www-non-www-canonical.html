Redirect Chains, www vs Non-www, and the URL Google Actually Indexes

Published 2026-11-23 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

One page, four addresses

A small business site is usually reachable at four addresses before anyone opens a settings page: http://example.com.au, https://example.com.au, http://www.example.com.au and https://www.example.com.au. Google's guide to consolidating duplicate URLs lists exactly this situation — a home page reachable several ways — as the reason to pick one preferred address and redirect the others to it.

Nothing breaks if you stay silent. That same guide says none of these methods are required, and Google will work out which version is objectively best on its own. What you lose is control: control of the address customers see in a result, and control of the address that collects your links and your Search Console clicks.

Permanent and temporary tell Google different things

Redirect type is not a technicality. Google's redirect guide sets out the split:

  • Permanent — 301, 308, or a zero-second meta refresh. Googlebot follows it, and the indexing pipeline uses it as a signal that the redirect target should be canonical.
  • Temporary — 302, 303, 307, or a meta refresh with a delay. Googlebot still follows it, but the target is not treated as canonical, and the old URL can keep appearing in Search.

The expensive mistake is a temporary redirect on a permanent move: a new domain, a rebrand, http to https, or a renamed page. That is the standard way a site ends up with a dead URL still ranking months after the move.

Redirects that point at other redirects

Chains arrive in batches. Someone adds www, later moves to https, later renames a page — and now http://www.example.com.au/services answers with a hop, to a hop, to a hop. Every hop is another request for a real visitor and for Googlebot. Google's guidance is a server-side redirect configured once, pointing straight at the final destination, rather than rules stacked on top of each other. Server-side redirects also have the highest chance of being interpreted correctly, well ahead of meta refresh and JavaScript.

How to see your own chains in five minutes

curl -sIL http://example.com.au | grep -Ei "^(HTTP/|location:)"

Each HTTP/ line is one hop and each Location: is where it points. Run that for the http, non-www and www versions of your home page and your three busiest service pages. The healthy reading is a single hop to a 301, then a final 200 — all of it landing on the same hostname with the same trailing-slash convention.

Keep the signals pointing one way

Redirects stack with everything else, so the free wins are consistency: link to your preferred address internally instead of whichever variant is in the menu, list the canonical host in sitemap.xml, and put a self-referential rel="canonical" on the canonical page itself. Two things not to do: don't use robots.txt to sort out canonicalisation, because Google can still index a disallowed URL without its content, and don't reach for the URL removal tool, which hides every version of that URL.
