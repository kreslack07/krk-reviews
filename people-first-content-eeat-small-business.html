E-E-A-T Without the Myth: What Google Asks of a Small Business Website

Published 2026-11-21 · By KRK Digital Products (ABN 71 770 320 895, Australia)

Start with what Google actually says, because most E-E-A-T writing gets it backwards. Experience, expertise, authoritativeness and trustworthiness are not a specific ranking factor. There is no score to raise. Google's guidance describes E-E-A-T as a way of summarising the mix of factors its systems use to identify helpful content, and adds that of the four, trust is the most important.

What that leaves a small business owner is not a set of tricks but three questions Google's documentation recommends asking about every page: who created it, how it was created, and why it exists.

Who wrote this?

If a reader cannot tell who wrote a page, they cannot judge whether to believe it. Google's guidance encourages accurate authorship — bylines where readers expect them, linking to something giving the author's background. For most small sites that means a real About page and a named author on service pages, not a stock photo and a first name.

The reverse is the part people skip: fabricating that information is deception, and Google names AI-generated headshots, made-up names and false credentials as examples. Deception makes a page untrustworthy and signals low quality. It matters most where content can affect health, finances or safety — Google calls these "Your Money or Your Life" topics, and gives extra weight to strong E-E-A-T there.

How was it created?

Readers and raters look for evidence of work. For a review that means how many products were tested, what the results were, how the tests ran, and photographs showing the work happened. If automation or AI generates content substantially, disclose it: say what role automation served and why. Google treats disclosures as helpful, not penalised.

Why does the page exist?

That is the load-bearing question. Google states the "why" should be that you create content primarily to help people — content that stays useful to a visitor who arrived directly. If the real reason is attracting search visits, the guidance is blunt: that is not what its systems reward, and using automation to mass-produce content for that purpose violates its spam policy on scaled content abuse.

Questions worth running over your own pages

Google publishes its own self-assessment, and part of it maps directly onto small business sites:

  • Does the page provide original information, analysis or first-hand experience, or does it summarise what others already published?
  • Does it add substantial value compared with the other pages ranking for the same query?
  • Would you bookmark it, share it with a friend, or recommend it?
  • Are there easily-verified factual errors — old prices, closed hours, a service you no longer offer?
  • Does the page look carefully produced, or hasty?
  • Would you expect to see it cited by a professional reference?

And the warning signs from Google's own list: writing to a word count because you heard Google prefers one (it does not); changing page dates to look fresh without changing anything substantial; and publishing across many topics hoping something sticks.

What actually moves the needle

For a small business the credibility is usually already in the building — years of completed jobs, certifications, warranties, real customer photographs, a named person who answers the phone. E-E-A-T work is turning that material into page content and clean authorship metadata: an author page, real project photos with descriptive alt text, prices and hours that match reality, service pages that say what you do and for whom.
